Experience the sound, not just the numbers.

Noise reports for wind farms often rely on sound pressure levels expressed in A-weighted decibels [dBA]. These values are essential for acoustic analysis, but they can be difficult to interpret without a technical background.

None of the noise regulations take into consideration the main characteristic of wind turbine noise- Amplitude Modulation.

SonicWind offers another way to engage with predicted sound: by listening.

Explore simulated turbine noise from different positions and compare it with familiar environmental sounds to better understand the role of listening position and acoustic context.

Built on acoustic modelling.
Designed for human understanding.

SonicWind is built on an advanced physics-based wind turbine noise model. Rather than relying on the interpertation of previous recordings, the model produces simulated audio signals for specified environmental scenarios.

The underlying model framework has been validated against observations and measurements from a real site.

SonicWind aims to make wind farm noise predictions easier to experience and discuss. It is intended to complement quantitative acoustic analysis, not replace site measurements, professional judgement or formal regulatory noise assessments.

From one turbine to your wind farm.

The current demonstration is an example of more flexible platform for exploring the predicted wind turbine sound.

SonicWind is intended to support tailored scenarios, multiple turbines and site-specific conditions. Depending on the wind turbine model and scenario, relevant inputs may include turbine characteristics, listening position, distance, wind direction, wind speed and atmospheric stability conditions.

The aim is to help users to have an experience of the sound of the proposed wind farm in different scenarios.

Clearer conversations about wind energy.

Wind&Sound was created to help bridge the gap between wind energy projects and the people who want to understand their potential impacts.

We believe technical information should be communicated with clarity and transparency. By making predicted turbine noise audible, we want to help people engage with acoustic information in a more accessible way—without hiding the assumptions, limitations or uncertainty behind a simulation.

Our goal is not to tell people what they should hear or think. It is to give them another way to explore the evidence.
